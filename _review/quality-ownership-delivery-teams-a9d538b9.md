A persistent illusion haunts software leadership: that quality is something done *to* code by specialists downstream, rather than built into it by the people who write it. For decades, organisations erected Testing Centres of Excellence — centralised gatekeeping functions whose mandate was to catch what developers had not bothered to prevent. The gates are still standing. The quality is still missing. According to the Tricentis 2025 Quality Transformation Report, which surveyed 2,750 organisations across ten countries, 45% explicitly prioritise delivery speed over quality; only 13% say quality comes first. More revealingly, 63% admit to deploying code without fully testing it. This is not a tactical concession under deadline pressure — it is the revealed preference of an industry that has externalised accountability so comprehensively it has forgotten what accountability feels like. The quality crisis is not accidental. It was designed.

## Speed's Hidden Invoice

The financial argument for treating quality as optional is, to put it charitably, eccentric. According to the Tricentis 2025 Quality Transformation Report, 40% of organisations estimate that poor software quality costs them more than $1 million every year; in financial services, nearly half report annual losses exceeding $5 million. These are not outliers absorbing the industry's misfortune — they are the median experience of a sector that has convinced itself that shipping faster saves money. The risk picture is equally unambiguous. Two-thirds of organisations surveyed by Tricentis consider themselves likely to suffer an outage or major disruption within the next twelve months, with nearly a quarter rating themselves "extremely at risk." That self-assessment sits alongside the admission that 63% deploy without full testing — a consistency that is almost admirable in its honesty. Organisations know they are exposed; they have simply decided that the cost of slowing down feels larger than the cost of breaking down. This calculus is wrong, and demonstrably so. The speed-quality trade-off has become an article of faith in engineering culture precisely because its falsity is deferred: the under-tested release ships on Friday; the incident fires on Saturday; the post-mortem blames the infrastructure. Quality's invoice always arrives, but rarely at the desk where the decision to skip testing was made. That distance — between the choice and its consequences — is the structural condition that any serious leadership reset must close. Any reform that does not make the cost visible, real-time, and attributable to the team that incurred it will fail in exactly the same way its predecessors did.

## The Silo That Built the Problem

The centralised Testing Centre of Excellence was a reasonable idea for a waterfall world, where code moved in large batches and hand-offs were explicit. Agile destroyed that world and left the testing model standing in the rubble. By the time delivery teams were deploying multiple times per day, a downstream QA function had become a structural absurdity — a quality gate for a pipeline that no longer pauses. The Capgemini World Quality Report 2024-25, drawn from 1,775 respondents across 33 countries, documents the collapse in blunt terms: use of centralised Testing Centres of Excellence has fallen 70% year-on-year, while 40% of organisations now embed quality engineers directly inside agile delivery teams. What organisations are abandoning is not quality — it is the organisational fiction that quality can be added to software after the fact by people who did not write it. The same Capgemini survey names the real constraint. Seventy per cent of respondents identify "Quality Engineering skills" — not headcount, not tooling, not budget line — as the single most critical organisational requirement. Ownership is a capability question first, a structural question second. Disbanding a centralised QA function and distributing its headcount across squads achieves nothing if those squads treat their embedded engineer as an outsider with a clipboard rather than a craft partner with a stake in the outcome. The failure mode of the new model is identical to the failure mode of the old one: quality treated as someone else's problem, just with a shorter commute between the someone-elses.

## Embedded, Measured, Owned

What structural reform actually requires is infrastructure for accountability — specifically, the feedback loops and quality metrics that most organisations currently lack. The Tricentis 2025 Quality Transformation Report finds that a third of organisations cite poor communication and weak feedback loops between developers and testers as a primary barrier to quality; nearly a third admit they lack clear quality metrics entirely. Embedding a quality engineer in a delivery team without giving that team visibility into production outcomes is merely relocating the problem one desk to the left. The automation layer is where this becomes tractable at scale. According to Capgemini's World Quality Report 2024-25, 68% of organisations have moved beyond experimentation and are actively deploying generative AI in quality engineering; 72% of those cite faster automation as the primary benefit, and 29% have fully integrated GenAI into test automation pipelines. The significance here is not the automation itself — test automation has existed for decades — but that it dramatically lowers the cost of building feedback infrastructure. A delivery team that once could not afford to maintain a comprehensive automated suite, because the maintenance burden swallowed the benefit, can now regenerate and adapt that suite with material assistance from tooling. The bottleneck shifts from test creation to test design judgment, which is precisely the skill that embedded quality engineers should supply. None of this is painless. Shifting quality ownership to delivery teams means accepting that some teams will own it badly, at least for a while, and that the remediation will be visible rather than absorbed into a centralised function's backlog. Transparency is not an unfortunate side effect of this model — it is the point. A quality crisis made visible is one leadership can actually fix. The invisible kind compounds quietly until the outage fixes it instead.
