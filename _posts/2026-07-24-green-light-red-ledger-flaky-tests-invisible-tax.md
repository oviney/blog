A software engineer who dismisses a failing CI pipeline as "probably flaky" is not cutting corners—she is making a statistically well-informed decision. That quiet act of professional resignation, multiplied across thousands of engineers and hundreds of daily builds, is how flaky tests drain engineering budgets: not with a sudden catastrophe but with the gentle persistence of a dripping tap that nobody has been asked to fix. The argument here is blunter than most engineering post-mortems allow: flaky tests are not a nuisance to be managed with auto-retry scripts but a quantifiable payroll cost, a signal-to-noise crisis, and a structural defect in how software quality is measured. The numbers, once examined, make much of that case—though, as we shall see, the same numbers make a serious case for the opposite conclusion, and it deserves an answer.

## The Payroll Cost of Phantom Failures

At Atlassian, reruns caused by flaky tests waste over 150,000 developer-hours a year in the Jira backend repository alone, according to Atlassian's engineering blog. In the Jira Frontend repository, flakiness has been responsible for as much as 21% of master build failures—tests that disagree with themselves from one run to the next, rather than code that is genuinely broken. No other category of engineering waste operates so openly and at such scale with so little executive scrutiny. A peer-reviewed industrial case study presented at ICST 2024, conducted on a commercial project of roughly 30 developers and about a million lines of code, put the cost in terms any CFO can read without a tutorial in continuous integration: developers spent up to 1.28% of their working time simply repairing flaky tests, a recurring charge the authors priced at roughly $2,250 a month. That is a modest-sounding percentage attached to a bill that never stops arriving, for work that ships nothing.

## A Signal-to-Noise Catastrophe

Google's engineers found that around 16% of all tests in the company's codebase carry some degree of flakiness—roughly one in seven of the tests their engineers write will occasionally fail for reasons unconnected to any change in the code. More corrosive is what happens to the signal. Writing in 2016, Google's John Micco reported that about 84% of observed transitions from passing to failing *involve* a flaky test, against an underlying rate of roughly 1.5% of all test runs returning a flaky result. Involvement is not the same thing as causation, and Micco was explicit that the damage runs in both directions: because the false-positive rate is high enough to make dismissal rational, genuine failures get waved away as flakes too. Engineers, being empiricists, eventually update their priors and stop trusting the signal. This erosion of trust is the compounding cost that balance sheets do not capture. Teams that have learnt to distrust their test suites begin shipping with lower confidence in quality gates, schedule additional manual review sessions, and—most damagingly—delay deployments until someone with institutional knowledge can intuit whether a failure "looks real." The hidden overhead has been quietly offloaded from the pipeline to the engineer's nervous system. It is worth adding that Google's figure is now a decade old and has not been publicly updated.

## The Arithmetic of Investigation

The ICST 2024 case study also produced one of the starkest pieces of unit economics in recent software engineering research: manual investigation of a flaky pipeline failure costs $5.67 in developer time, against 0.02 cents for an automatic rerun. The gap is not a rounding difference; it is roughly four orders of magnitude. This arithmetic explains why silent auto-retry has become the de facto industry response. It is cheaper, in the narrow sense, to rerun a test three times and declare it stable than to pay a senior engineer to diagnose why it failed. Microsoft researchers studying flaky tests in a large-scale industrial setting found that when they re-ran flaky tests locally 100 times, 86% of them proved flaky only in the CI pipeline and never on a developer's machine. When the failure cannot be reproduced where the engineer is sitting, root-cause analysis becomes not merely expensive but epistemically hostile—the engineer is hunting a ghost. A large fraction of flaky tests are therefore never fixed; they are quarantined, muted, or deleted, taking whatever signal they once carried with them into the void.

## The Case Against This Article

Here is where intellectual honesty requires a detour, because the strongest objection to the argument above is sitting inside its own footnotes. The Munich team that produced the $5.67 figure drew the opposite conclusion from it. Finding reruns negligibly cheap by comparison, they recommended shifting effort *away* from manual investigation and *toward* automatically rerunning tests. Atlassian built retry-based detection with automated quarantine and reports recovering thousands of builds a quarter. Google, in the very post the 84% comes from, lists rerunning and automatic quarantine among its own mitigations. Every organisation cited here evaluated the trade and came down on the side of the retry.

On unit cost alone they are right, and a defence of retry has to be answered rather than dismissed. Engineering time is the scarce input; spending it on a defect class that will not reproduce on the machine you are debugging from is a poor use of it. The honest version of the argument is therefore not that retry is a cop-out, but that it is *unbounded*. A team that retries without a stated ceiling has no way to notice the moment when the quarantine has quietly become the test strategy. The number worth arguing about is not whether to retry but what share of a suite may be under retry before the suite has stopped being a test of anything.

## Clustered Roots, Concentrated Fixes

The news is not uniformly grim. A 2025 empirical analysis of flaky test failures across open-source Java projects found that flaky tests tend not to fail alone: 75% of them belong to a cluster of tests that fail together and share a root cause, with a mean cluster size of 13.5 tests. The predominant causes the authors identified were intermittent networking issues and instabilities in external dependencies—and they argued explicitly that earlier studies naming asynchronous operations as the leading cause may have been skewed by treating flaky failures as isolated events rather than correlated ones. The clustering is the actionable finding: because root causes are shared, a single fix can retire a dozen tests at once, which changes the economics of repair considerably.

Engineering teams that treat flakiness as background radiation—something to be tolerated rather than measured, bounded, and eliminated—are paying a recurring tax on every deployment they ship. The most interesting question is not whether flaky tests are expensive; the arithmetic above settles that. It is why so few teams put a number on the tolerance. Retrying is the right call at the margin, and it makes the dashboard green, the standup short, and the quarterly metrics tidy. What it does not do is tell you when you have stopped testing and started hoping. The dripping tap has a way of flooding the basement—and by then, the plumber charges considerably more than the pipe was worth.

## Correction

**Correction (July 25th 2026):** An earlier version of this article, published on July 24th 2026, contained a number of errors of fact and attribution. They were substantial enough to warrant setting them out in full.

- **The cost of an automatic rerun was wrong by a factor of 100.** The article gave it as $0.02 against $5.67 for a manual investigation, "a 280-fold gap." The source states 0.02 *cents*. The true gap is roughly four orders of magnitude.
- **Reference 1 was credited to the wrong author.** It was attributed to "Parry, J. et al." The paper is by Leinen, Elsner, Pretschner, Stahlbauer, Sailer and Juergens. Owain Parry is an author of reference 5, not this one.
- **Reference 5 was given a title that does not exist.** It was listed as "Empirical Study of Flaky Test Co-Failure Groups in 22 Java Projects." The paper is "Systemic Flakiness: An Empirical Analysis of Co-Occurring Flaky Test Failures."
- **A claim that 45% of flakiness traces to asynchronous wait errors has been removed.** It was attributed to reference 5, which reports no such finding and in fact argues against naming asynchronous operations as the leading cause. The recommendation built on it—a sprint on async test hygiene—has been removed with it.
- **The article did not acknowledge that its primary source reached the opposite conclusion.** The ICST 2024 authors used their own cost figures to argue *for* shifting effort toward automatic reruns. The article cited those figures to argue that auto-retry is "an anaesthetic, not a cure," without noting the disagreement. A new section, "The Case Against This Article," now puts that counter-argument.
- **Google's finding was overstated.** The article said 84% of pass-to-fail transitions are "caused by flaky tests rather than genuine code defects." The source says such transitions *involve* a flaky test, and notes that genuine failures are also wrongly dismissed as flakes. The underlying 1.5% flaky-run rate, omitted originally, has been added, as has the date of the finding. The line describing an alert system that "cries wolf 84% of the time" has been removed.
- **Atlassian's figure was hardened.** "As much as 21%... in the past" was rendered as a flat, present-tense "21% of all master build failures." A further sentence claiming that "one in five emergency Slack notifications is, strictly speaking, a lie" has been removed: the source says nothing about notifications and the inference was unsupported.
- **A Microsoft reproduction-rate figure has been replaced.** A claim that flaky failures reproduce in only 25–43% of cases after 500 runs was sourced to a vendor blog and could not be verified against the underlying study. It has been replaced with a directly sourced finding from Lam et al. (ISSTA 2019).
- **Two derived figures have been removed.** Estimates of 6,600 developer-hours and 3.75 developer-years depended on an assumed working year that the article never stated. The sourced percentage and monthly cost are given instead.
- **The chart has been removed.** It presented "genuine defect" shares of 16% and 79% for Google and the Jira Frontend repository. Neither figure appears in either source; both were derived by subtracting the flaky-test share from 100, and the 16% collided confusingly with an unrelated real figure—the share of Google's tests carrying some flakiness. A replacement chart will be published only if the underlying figures can be made genuinely comparable.

The errors originated in an automated drafting pipeline whose quality checks validated the article's structure but never verified its citations against the cited sources. That gap is being closed. We regret the errors.
